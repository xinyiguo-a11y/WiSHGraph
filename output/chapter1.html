1 Project Introduction

1.1 What is Cyber-Wishing?

Cyber-wishing refers to online practices in which users seek luck, protection, success, wealth, health, romance, safety or emotional reassurance through digital media objects and platform interactions. It appears through reposted lucky images, comments such as “receive good luck,” deity-themed avatars, blessing wallpapers, “Offer Bodhisattva” memes, online divination tools and meme-based platform challenges. These practices draw on older forms of wishing, blessing, divination, amulet use and deity worship, while adapting them to the participatory and technical environments of digital platforms. Cyber-wishing is therefore not simply the digital reproduction of traditional ritual: images, symbols, phrases and ritual actions are selected, modified, circulated and recombined through platform practices such as commenting, reposting, saving, sharing and changing avatars. This project understands cyber-wishing as a culturally meaningful form of platformed ritual and digital folklore, in which everyday uncertainties become linked to symbolic acts of hope, luck-seeking, participation and affective reassurance (Bell, 1992; Blank, 2009; Shifman, 2014).

1.2 What Does This Project Aim to Model?

This project models cyber-wishing as a relational cultural phenomenon. The term cyber-wishing refers to the broader cultural phenomenon investigated by the project, while CyberRitualCase is the formal ontological class used to represent an individual instance of that phenomenon. Rather than treating a meme, image, user or ritual symbol as the sole unit of analysis, the ontology places CyberRitualCase at its centre. Each case can be connected to Subject (who participates), PerformativeAction (what action is carried out), Platform (where it circulates), SecularPressure (what uncertainty or desire motivates it), RemediatedSignifier (what digital object or symbolic form is used), RitualSymbol (what ritual elements appear within that signifier), Archetype (what broader cultural or ritual source is invoked), RemediationStrategy (how existing cultural material is transformed), and AffectiveOutcome (what affective result is associated with the case). Through these relations, cyber-wishing can be represented not as a collection of isolated memes, but as a structured network connecting subjects, pressures, symbolic materials, cultural references, platform actions, transformations and affective outcomes. The project therefore uses ontology as an explicit representation of a shared conceptual structure and as a basis for queryable cultural analysis (Gruber, 1993; Studer et al., 1998).

2 Rationale and Cultural Background

2.1 Historical Roots in Chinese Folk Religion and Blessing Practices

Cyber-wishing grows from China’s long cultural background of folk religion, blessing practices and everyday ritual life. Many online symbols carry familiar ritual meanings before they enter digital circulation. Caishen is associated with wealth, business, trade and financial luck. On the fifth day of the Lunar New Year, people traditionally welcome Caishen, send away poverty and open businesses with the hope of attracting prosperity. In online spaces, this ritual logic appears through Caishen images on WeChat Moments and wealth-related blessing posts. Mazu, widely known as a protector of sea travel and safe journeys, also enters digital practice through reposted images, online divination and “cyber moon blocks” before travel. These examples show how older ritual figures become digital materials for contemporary wishing practices. They give online users a cultural vocabulary for making wishes about money, safety, romance, health and luck intelligible (Chau, 2006).

2.2 Contemporary Pressures and Uncertainty

Cyber-wishing also responds to contemporary uncertainty. In the dataset, users turn to cyber-wishing when facing academic pressure, job insecurity, financial anxiety, health concerns, romantic uncertainty, travel safety, or the randomness of games and fandom consumption. A gamer may place a desired character image beside an iPad and play the song Good Luck Comes before drawing cards. A fan may “pray” to a celebrity image before opening albums in the hope of receiving a preferred photocard. Couples may ask others to comment “99,” which sounds like “long-lasting” in Chinese, as a small public wish for romantic continuity. These practices turn uncertain outcomes into symbolic actions that users can perform, share and repeat.

2.3 Platform Circulation and Meme Transformation

Cyber-wishing takes shape through platform circulation. Likes, reposts, comments, saves, avatars, hashtags and recommendation systems participate in the formation of these practices. A blessing may develop into a repost chain, a lucky image may circulate as a shareable meme, and a ritual phrase may settle into a repeated comment formula. For example, “receive good luck” turns blessing into a comment action. Online divination also changes ritual consultation into an interface that users can click, screenshot and share. Platform affordances shape the form, circulation and public reach of cyber-wishing (Bucher & Helmond, 2018).

2.4 Why Ontology is Suitable

Ontology is suitable for this project because cyber-wishing is organised through relations rather than isolated attributes. A spreadsheet can record individual observations, but an ontology can explicitly represent how a case connects a subject, an action, a platform, a pressure, a digital signifier, a cultural archetype, a remediation process and an affective outcome. The project therefore moves from cultural description toward queryable knowledge representation. Following principles associated with eXtreme Design, the modelling process is guided by competency questions, reusable modelling patterns and iterative refinement. Rather than treating the ontology as a fixed structure designed in advance, the project develops the model through repeated movement between case analysis, conceptual abstraction, ontology design and testing (Presutti et al., 2009).

3 Theoretical Framework

3.1 Adapted 4P Framework

This project initially draws on Rhodes’ 4P model of creativity as a conceptual scaffold for organising cyber-wishing. In the original model, creativity is approached through four connected dimensions: Person, Process, Product and Press (Rhodes, 1961). For the purposes of cyber-wishing, Person concerns the users who participate in wishing practices; Process concerns the actions through which wishing is performed; Product concerns the symbolic or media objects through which wishing becomes materially visible; and Press concerns the social, emotional, cultural and situational conditions surrounding the practice. Because cyber-wishing takes place within technically structured social-media environments, Platform is treated as an additional explicit dimension.

The adapted 4P framework functions as an initial analytical scaffold rather than the final ontology structure. During conceptual abstraction and ontology development, its dimensions were refined into more precise representational categories: Person is represented primarily through Subject; Process through PerformativeAction; Product through RemediatedSignifier; Press through SecularPressure; and Platform remains an explicit modelling dimension. Additional classes, including RitualSymbol, Archetype, RemediationStrategy and AffectiveOutcome, were introduced because the 4P structure alone could not adequately represent the symbolic transformation and affective dimensions observed in the cases. The 4P framework therefore provides the conceptual starting point, while the final ontology develops a more specialised model for cyber-wishing.

3.2 Digital Folklore

Digital folklore explains cyber-wishing as a vernacular practice created and circulated by ordinary platform users. Their meanings depend on communal recognition. Users understand that commenting “receive good luck,” reposting a koi image, or sharing an “Offer Bodhisattva” meme performs a culturally recognisable act of wishing. Cyber-wishing therefore belongs to everyday online folklore, where users collectively produce and reshape symbolic forms (Howard, 2008).

3.3 Ritual Theory

Ritual theory helps explain why cyber-wishing carries ritual logic even when it appears humorous or informal. It involves repeated actions, symbolic objects and expected transformations. These actions carry ritual meaning through their timing, repetition and symbolic intention. They create a small symbolic order through which users move from anxiety toward hope, reassurance or imagined protection (Turner, 1969).

3.4 Meme Theory and Platform Affordances

Meme theory explains how cyber-wishing symbols become repeatable cultural forms that can be adapted across different online communities. A deity image, lucky phrase, or blessing template can be copied, modified and circulated as users adjust it to specific pressures and platform contexts. Platform affordance theory explains the technical conditions of this circulation. Comments, reposts, likes, saves, avatars, hashtags and recommendation systems shape how cyber-wishing is performed and made visible. Cyber-wishing is therefore both a form of content and a platform action shaped by the technical and social features of digital media (Hutchby, 2001; Milner, 2016).

3.5 Affective Practice

Cyber-wishing also works as an affective practice. Users share symbols while managing feelings attached to uncertainty, pressure and desire. These practices help users externalise anxiety, produce hope, share uncertainty and turn pressure into humour. In the dataset, this appears through anxiety relief, confidence building, emotional support, romantic reassurance and communal encouragement. Cyber-wishing connects personal emotion with public participation, giving users a way to make uncertainty socially visible and emotionally manageable (Ahmed, 2004).

4 Methodology

The project follows an iterative knowledge-modelling workflow that moves between cultural case analysis, conceptual abstraction, ontology design, multimodal extraction, instantiation and SPARQL-based evaluation. Rather than assuming that either the ontology or the dataset was fully defined in advance, the methodology treats modelling as an iterative process. Initial cyber-wishing cases informed the conceptual categories and Competency Questions; these were used to construct a preliminary T-Box. The developing ontology then guided the structure of the extraction dataset, while observations from the extracted cases were used to refine the model.

Overall workflow: Domain cases → Conceptual abstraction → Competency Questions → T-Box → Multimodal extraction → Structured dataset → A-Box → SPARQL → Cultural interpretation.

The aim is to construct structured cyber-wishing cases that can populate and test the ontology. Descriptive counts may be used to illustrate patterns within the selected corpus, but statistical inference and generalisation beyond the dataset remain outside the scope of the project.

4.1 Domain Analysis and Case Selection

The project began with the examination of representative examples of cyber-wishing drawn from Chinese-language digital culture. Materials include lucky images, blessing posts, comment rituals, deity-themed avatars, repost chains, meme templates, celebrity-based wishing practices and online divination interfaces. Initial cases were used not only as data but also as conceptual probes. By comparing cases that differed in cultural source, symbolic form and platform context, the project identified recurring representational dimensions that could support a general model of cyber-wishing. This case-driven analysis informed the development of the ontology’s central concepts and relations.

4.2 Conceptual Abstraction and Competency Questions

Following the initial case analysis, recurring dimensions were abstracted into a conceptual model. The project then formulated Competency Questions (CQs) to specify what the ontology should be able to represent and answer. These questions concern who performs a cyber-wishing action, what action is carried out, where the case circulates, what secular pressure motivates the practice, what remediated signifier is used, what ritual symbols it contains, what cultural archetype is invoked, how the signifier has been transformed and what affective outcome is associated with the case. The Competency Questions therefore serve as functional requirements for ontology design: if answering a question requires a particular entity or relation, that requirement must be represented within the T-Box.

4.3 Ontology Modelling and T-Box Construction

The conceptual model and Competency Questions were formalised in Protégé or WebProtégé. The central class is CyberRitualCase, which represents an individual cyber-wishing case as a relational unit. The ontology includes classes such as Subject, PerformativeAction, Platform, SecularPressure, RemediatedSignifier, RitualSymbol, Archetype, RemediationStrategy and AffectiveOutcome. Object properties specify the semantic relations among these entities. For example, a CyberRitualCase may circulate on a Platform, be triggered by a SecularPressure, employ a RemediatedSignifier, undergo a RemediationStrategy and resolve into an AffectiveOutcome. The model was refined iteratively through hierarchy design, domain and range checking, relation testing and comparison with real cases. Where appropriate, selected relations were aligned with reusable ontology patterns and external vocabularies.

4.4 Multimodal Extraction and Structured Dataset

After the preliminary T-Box had been established, cyber-wishing materials were processed through AI-assisted multimodal extraction combined with manual validation. Because the source materials are primarily image-text objects, relevant information may be distributed across visual elements, written captions, platform context, interface features, hashtags, emojis and culturally implied references. AI-assisted extraction was used to identify candidate information, while manual checking was required to correct errors, interpret ambiguous cultural references, standardise terminology and map observations to ontology categories.

The extracted information was organised into a structured CSV dataset. The dataset functions as a bridge between raw digital-cultural material and ontology instantiation. Its fields correspond to ontology-relevant dimensions, including information about the case, subject, action, platform, pressure, signifier, ritual symbols, archetype, remediation strategy, affective outcome and source evidence. The dataset was therefore not treated as an independent classification scheme; it was developed in dialogue with the ontology and refined as modelling decisions evolved.

4.5 A-Box Instantiation

Selected cases from the structured dataset were instantiated as individuals in the ontology. Each case became an instance of CyberRitualCase and was connected through object properties to relevant instances of Subject, PerformativeAction, Platform, SecularPressure, RemediatedSignifier, RitualSymbol, Archetype, RemediationStrategy and AffectiveOutcome. This stage transformed the ontology from a conceptual schema into a populated knowledge graph. In this project, the T-Box defines the conceptual structure and permitted relations, while the A-Box contains the concrete cyber-wishing cases represented through that structure. Together they form the queryable WishGraph knowledge graph.

4.6 SPARQL Testing and Cultural Interpretation

The populated ontology was tested through SPARQL queries derived from the Competency Questions. This stage evaluates whether the ontology can retrieve the relations it was designed to represent. Queries examine, for example, how subjects, actions, platforms, pressures, signifiers, archetypes, remediation strategies and affective outcomes are connected across individual cases. SPARQL results are treated as structured evidence for cultural interpretation rather than as statistically representative findings. Descriptive patterns within the selected corpus may reveal recurring relations, but the project does not claim that a small purposively selected dataset represents the full population of cyber-wishing practices. The analytical process therefore moves from query result → relational pattern → cultural interpretation.
