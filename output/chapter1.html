1 Project Introduction

1.1 What is Cyber-Wishing?

Cyber-wishing refers to online practices in which users seek luck and protection through digital media objects and platform interactions. These practices also address success and wealth, while health, romance, safety, and emotional reassurance appear across different cases. Common forms include lucky images, blessing posts, symbolic avatars, meme-based rituals, and digital divination.

These practices draw on older forms of wishing and blessing. Divination, amulet use, and deity worship also inform their development in digital environments. Platform participation and technical features reshape how these materials circulate. Images and ritual actions may be selected or modified, while phrases and symbols move through different forms of platform participation.

This project understands cyber-wishing as a culturally meaningful form of platformed ritual and digital folklore. Everyday uncertainty becomes connected to symbolic acts of hope and luck-seeking, while participation also carries affective reassurance (Bell, 1992; Blank, 2009; Shifman, 2014).

1.2 What Does This Project Aim to Model?

This project models cyber-wishing as a relational cultural phenomenon.

The term cyber-wishing refers to the broader cultural phenomenon investigated by the project. CyberRitualCase serves as the formal ontological class for an individual instance of that phenomenon.

The ontology represents each case through several connected dimensions. These dimensions cover the participant and the action performed, together with the platform context and the pressure associated with the practice.

The model also records the digital signifier through which a case is expressed and the ritual elements connected to it. Broader cultural sources are represented through Archetype, while RemediationStrategy captures transformation in digital circulation. AffectiveOutcome records the emotional or affective result associated with the practice.

These dimensions form a structured representation of cyber-wishing. They connect participation and pressure with symbolic material, cultural reference, platform activity, transformation, and affective outcome.

Ontology provides an explicit representation of this conceptual structure and supports queryable cultural analysis (Gruber, 1993; Studer et al., 1998).

2 Rationale and Cultural Background

2.1 Historical Roots in Folk Religion and Blessing Practices

Cyber-wishing draws on a longer history of folk religion and blessing practices. Divination and everyday ritual life also shape its cultural background. Many symbols already carry recognisable ritual meanings before they enter digital environments.

Figures associated with prosperity, protection, health, or good fortune often retain these cultural associations in digital circulation. Their images can appear in blessing posts, memes, divination interfaces, and other shareable forms.

These practices provide cultural background for the wider phenomenon of cyber-wishing. Older ritual figures and symbolic systems become reusable materials in digital settings, where established meanings are adapted to contemporary concerns (Chau, 2006).

2.2 Contemporary Pressures and Uncertainty

Cyber-wishing is closely connected to contemporary uncertainty.

Within the selected dataset, different forms of pressure provide the context for cyber-wishing practices. Academic and financial concerns appear alongside health-related or relational uncertainty. Other cases develop around aspirations shaped by platform culture.

These pressures often become connected to symbolic or performative actions. Cyber-wishing gives uncertainty a form that users can act on and share. Repetition and visualisation also shape the emotional management of that uncertainty.

2.3 Platform Circulation and Meme Transformation

Cyber-wishing takes shape through platform circulation.

Platform interactions influence how wishing practices are performed and circulated. Reposting can extend the reach of a blessing, while comments can turn a symbolic phrase into a repeated participatory action. Images and other digital objects can also move between contexts as reusable forms.

Interface features and recommendation systems influence how these practices become visible and circulate. Platform affordances therefore shape both the performance and distribution of cyber-wishing (Bucher & Helmond, 2018).

2.4 Why Ontology is Suitable

An ontology is suitable for this project because cyber-wishing is organised through relations across multiple entities.

A spreadsheet can record individual observations. An ontology adds explicit connections between the elements that make up a cyber-wishing case. Participants and actions can be related to platform contexts, while pressures can be connected with symbolic material and cultural reference. Transformation and affective outcome become part of the same representation.

This structure supports queryable knowledge representation while retaining the cultural detail recorded in each case.

Following principles associated with eXtreme Design, competency questions guide the modelling process. Reusable modelling patterns inform the structure. Iterative refinement develops the ontology through comparison between case analysis and conceptual abstraction, while ontology design and testing continue that cycle (Presutti et al., 2009).

3 Theoretical Framework

3.1 Adapted 4P Framework

This project draws initially on Rhodes’ 4P model of creativity as a conceptual scaffold for organising cyber-wishing. In the original model, Person and Process form two dimensions of creativity, with Product and Press completing the framework (Rhodes, 1961).

Within this project, Person concerns users who participate in wishing practices. Process concerns the actions through which wishing is performed. Product refers to the symbolic or media objects that give the practice a visible form. Press covers the surrounding social and cultural conditions, together with emotional and situational factors.

Because cyber-wishing develops within technically structured social-media environments, Platform is treated as an additional modelling dimension.

The adapted 4P framework serves as an initial analytical scaffold for the ontology. During ontology development, its dimensions were refined into more precise representational categories.

In the final ontology, Person is represented through Subject, while Process is expressed through PerformativeAction. Product corresponds to RemediatedSignifier, and Press is represented through SecularPressure. Platform remains an explicit modelling dimension.

The ontology also includes categories for ritual content and broader cultural reference. Further dimensions capture digital transformation and affective outcome.

The 4P framework provides the conceptual starting point, and the final ontology develops a specialised model for cyber-wishing.

3.2 Digital Folklore

Digital folklore frames cyber-wishing as a vernacular practice created by ordinary platform users. Circulation changes these forms over time, while shared cultural recognition supports their meaning.

Wishing practices often rely on recognisable phrases, images, or symbolic references. Their repetition across platforms gives them a shared cultural form while leaving space for modification by individual users.

Cyber-wishing forms part of everyday online folklore. Users reproduce symbolic forms through participation, and circulation reshapes those forms across platform contexts (Howard, 2008).

3.3 Ritual Theory

Ritual theory helps account for the ritual logic carried by cyber-wishing across informal and digitally mediated forms. These practices organise action through repetition and symbolic intention.

Cyber-wishing often involves repeated actions and symbolic objects. Recognisable sequences organise the practice, while users anticipate movement from uncertainty toward reassurance or hope.

Its ritual character can emerge through repetition and symbolic intention. Performative action and culturally meaningful expectations of transformation also contribute to that ritual logic (Turner, 1969).

3.4 Meme Theory and Platform Affordances

Meme theory helps explain how cyber-wishing signifiers become repeatable cultural forms. Their adaptability supports circulation across different online communities.

Digital signifiers can be copied and modified as users respond to specific pressures. Their meanings may also shift with audience and platform context.

Platform affordance theory explains the technical conditions of these transformations. Interaction features shape participation and circulation, while platform interfaces influence how cyber-wishing becomes visible and performable.

Cyber-wishing operates as symbolic content and as platformed action shaped by the technical and social conditions of digital media (Hutchby, 2001; Milner, 2016).

3.5 Affective Practice

Cyber-wishing also operates as an affective practice.

Users circulate symbolic forms while managing emotions associated with pressure and uncertainty. Desire and anticipated outcomes also shape these practices. Hope, reassurance, and emotional comfort appear across the selected cases.

The specific affective outcome varies with the pressure and symbolic structure of each case. These outcomes cover concerns related to personal wellbeing and relationships, while other cases centre on academic or financial aspirations.

Cyber-wishing connects individual emotion with public participation. Symbolic and performative actions make private uncertainty socially visible and open it to collective negotiation (Ahmed, 2004).

4 Methodology

The project follows an iterative knowledge-modelling workflow. Cultural case analysis informs conceptual abstraction, which then feeds into ontology design. Multimodal extraction supplies structured data for instantiation, and SPARQL supports evaluation.

Initial cyber-wishing cases informed the conceptual categories and competency questions. These questions guided the construction of a preliminary T-Box. The ontology then shaped the extraction dataset, while observations from the extracted cases fed back into model refinement.

The workflow begins with domain cases and conceptual abstraction. Competency Questions guide T-Box construction, after which multimodal extraction produces a structured dataset. The process then moves into A-Box instantiation and SPARQL querying before cultural interpretation.

The project constructs structured cyber-wishing cases that populate and test the ontology. Descriptive counts illustrate patterns within the selected corpus. The analytical claims stay within that corpus and focus on relational interpretation.

4.1 Domain Analysis and Case Selection

The project began by examining representative examples of cyber-wishing across contemporary digital culture.

The material spans several platform environments and cultural contexts. It includes visual posts and participatory practices, together with meme-based content and digitally mediated ritual activities.

Initial cases served as data and as analytical examples. Comparison across cultural source and symbolic form helped identify recurring dimensions. Platform context supplied another point of comparison for the model.

This case-driven analysis informed the ontology’s central concepts and relations.

4.2 Conceptual Abstraction and Competency Questions

Following the initial case analysis, recurring dimensions were abstracted into a conceptual model.

The project then formulated Competency Questions (CQs) to specify the kinds of relations and entities that the ontology should represent. The questions address participation and platform context, together with the pressures and symbolic materials connected to each case.

Further questions concern cultural reference and digital transformation. Affective outcome forms another part of the query structure. Some queries also draw on subclass information to distinguish broader categories within the ontology.

The Competency Questions serve as functional requirements for ontology design. Each required entity or relation is represented within the T-Box.

4.3 Ontology Modelling and T-Box Construction

The conceptual model and Competency Questions were formalised in Protégé or WebProtégé.

The central class is CyberRitualCase, which represents an individual cyber-wishing case as a relational unit.

The ontology organises each case around several connected dimensions. These cover participation and platform context, while another part of the model records the pressure associated with the practice and the digital signifier through which it is expressed.

Symbolic content and broader cultural reference form another layer of the model. The ontology also captures transformation in digital circulation and the affective outcome associated with each case.

Hierarchy design and domain-range checking refined the model. Relation testing and comparison with real cases supported further revision.

Where appropriate, selected relations were aligned with reusable ontology patterns and external vocabularies, integrating WishGraph into a broader semantic modelling context.

4.4 Multimodal Extraction and Structured Dataset

After the preliminary T-Box was established, cyber-wishing materials were processed through AI-assisted multimodal extraction combined with manual validation.

The source materials are multimodal digital objects, including images and videos. Relevant information may appear in visual elements or written captions. Platform context and interface features add further evidence, while audio and other platform-specific cues can contribute to interpretation.

AI-assisted extraction identified candidate information. Manual checking corrected errors and interpreted ambiguous cultural references. It also standardised terminology and mapped observations to ontology categories.

The extracted information was organised into a structured CSV dataset.

The dataset functions as a bridge between raw digital-cultural material and ontology instantiation. Its fields record the main dimensions defined during ontology development and preserve the source information associated with each case.

The dataset was developed alongside the ontology and refined as modelling decisions evolved.

4.5 A-Box Instantiation

Selected cases from the structured dataset were instantiated as individuals in the ontology.

Each case became an instance of CyberRitualCase. The A-Box records the relevant participants and actions, together with platform context and associated pressure. It also represents symbolic material, cultural reference, transformation, and affective outcome.

This step transformed the ontology from a conceptual schema into a populated knowledge graph.

The T-Box defines the conceptual structure and the relations permitted by the ontology. The A-Box contains the concrete cyber-wishing cases represented through that structure. Together, they form the queryable WishGraph knowledge graph.

4.6 SPARQL Testing and Cultural Interpretation

The populated ontology was tested through SPARQL queries derived from the Competency Questions.

The queries test whether the ontology retrieves the relations specified during modelling. They examine connections across the main dimensions represented in the knowledge graph and support comparison between cases.

SPARQL results provide structured evidence for cultural interpretation. Descriptive patterns identify recurring relations within the selected corpus, and the project keeps its claims at that corpus level.

The analytical process moves from query results to relational patterns and then to cultural interpretation.
